Upsolving is finishing the problems you left unfinished, on your own, once the clock has stopped.

## Sitting a mock

Choose a past contest and set one three-hour timer. Sit it in one go, with only the resources the rules module lists. The value of a mock is the log, so keep one while you work. For every stretch on a problem, write the problem, the start minute, the end minute and the <Term id="verdict">verdict</Term> of the submission that closed the stretch. Local judging with official data, as the local-judging module describes, gives you those verdicts. Add one more line per problem while you are still in the contest: what you believed it needed, and where you stopped. You will check your upsolve against that line.

A problem accepted on its first submission needs nothing. A problem accepted after wrong answers has two attempts you can compare. ## Finding the smallest failing input

To see the second step work, take a small problem of our own. Given a list, find the length of its longest strictly increasing run of neighbors. Your contest attempt got wrong answer, and you write a brute force that tries every slice. The search below tries inputs shortest first, in a fixed order, and stops at the first one where the two programs disagree. Step through it, and try the "Length 2" preset, where the limit is too low to find anything.

<Figure caption="Searching for the smallest input on which the attempt and the brute force disagree, shortest inputs first">
  <StepThrough frames="visuals/first-failure.frames.json" preset="three" />
</Figure>

The search tried 14 inputs and found `[0, 0, 1]`: the brute force finds the run 0, 1 of length 2, and the attempt says 1. That input is three values long, which is why "Length 2" finds nothing. A search that finds nothing has not shown the attempt is right, only that the bug needs a bigger input. Shortest first matters. A three-value input can be traced by hand, while a failing input from a real judge may hold thousands of values, and you could not read it. The trace below runs the attempt on the small one. Watch `run` at the repeated 0.

<Figure caption="The wrong attempt traced on its smallest failing input">

At the repeated 0 the attempt sets `run` to 0 instead of 1, because a new run already holds the current value. On `[0, 1]` the bug never shows, since no value repeats, and on `[5]` the loop body never runs. The repaired version resets `run` to 1, and the last step of the search confirms that it matches the brute force on all 39 inputs tried.

## Retrying on a schedule

An upsolved problem feels solved, because you have just seen it done. The test is whether you can rebuild it later. A few days after the upsolve, retry the stalled problems from a blank file, using only the statement and your own notes. Whatever you can rebuild then is what you learned, and whatever you cannot is the next thing to upsolve.

## When to reach for this

Sit a mock when you want evidence about your pacing, and keep the log from the first minute. Upsolve straight afterward, while the contest is fresh, starting from the log's first step for each problem. Retry the stalled problems from a blank file a few days later, before the next mock.
