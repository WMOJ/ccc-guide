Suppose the clock reads minute 75 and you have just cleared the first <Term id="subtask">subtask</Term> of problem 4. The second subtask needs an idea you do not have yet, and 105 minutes remain. You can keep thinking about problem 4, or you can put it down and start a problem you have not opened. Neither choice is obviously right, and the contest does not pause while you decide. This lesson gives you a way to make that choice from evidence. The three hours are one budget of minutes, each subtask costs some of it, and you can review what remains at moments you set in advance.

Every number in this lesson's figures is made up for the example. No real contest or typical contestant is being described.

## Minutes add up per problem

Both plans in the figure come from one idea: the minutes you put into a problem accumulate, and a subtask clears once the total reaches its cost. Blocks on the same problem add together even when other problems sit between them. The short program below keeps two problems' totals and shows exactly that. On "Rotating", problem 1 receives 20 minutes, then problem 2 receives 25, which equals its cost, so it clears at once. "Short blocks" ends with `[20, 10]` and nothing cleared, which is what happened to plan B on "Stay pays". The model leaves out the cost of switching, which in a real contest is the minutes you need to reload a problem into your head. Longer blocks make that cost smaller relative to the work.

## Checkpoints

A checkpoint is a moment you fix in advance, such as the end of each block, when you stop and re-read what is left. You write down for every problem how many subtasks have cleared and your estimate for the next one. Then you compare each estimate with the minutes remaining. The figure below applies one simple rule: among the subtasks that fit in the remaining time, start the one with the smallest estimate. Try the three presets.

At minute 90, problem 3's 20 minutes is the smallest estimate, so the checkpoint picks problem 3, even though problem 5's first subtask is also within reach. At minute 165 only problem 5's 12 minutes fits in the 15 left. At minute 175, nothing fits, and the remaining five minutes go to re-checking the programs you already submitted on small cases, the way the stress-testing module describes.

The rule is one option, not the only one. It ignores that subtasks carry different marks, which the statement lists, and your estimates will be wrong by varying amounts. What the checkpoint guarantees is that you compare options instead of continuing on momentum. The estimates are your own guesses, so a checkpoint only helps as much as your estimates do. Timing yourself on past contests is how they get better, which the mock contests module takes up.

## When to reach for this

Set the checkpoints before the contest starts: after the reading pass, and after each block. Decide what triggers a move, such as a block reaching its estimate with no new idea. Then use the checkpoint to choose where the next block goes, from the estimates you wrote down and the minutes left.
