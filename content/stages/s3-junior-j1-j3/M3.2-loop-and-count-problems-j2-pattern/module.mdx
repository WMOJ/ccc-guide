Imagine a problem that reads the marks of several students and counts how many pass, meaning a mark of 50 or higher. The number of students, `n`, is read first, and then the marks follow, one per line.

Reading a list of items, counting how many meet a condition, and then outputting the result is the loop-and-count pattern. It builds on the loops of M1.6 and the accumulators of M2.4.

## The pattern: counter before, update inside

A J2 problem keeps `n` modest, and the loop does one comparison and at most one addition per item. The work grows in step with `n`: 5 marks take 5 comparisons, and 100,000 marks take 100,000, far below the 10^7 to 10^8 simple operations PyPy does in a second.

The counter is a running total. It starts at zero before the loop runs, and each time a mark passes, the loop adds one to it. After the loop ends, that variable holds the answer, and the program prints it.

## Choosing between at least and more than

The word in the problem statement decides the comparison. "50 or higher" needs `mark >= 50`. "Higher than 50" needs `mark > 50`. The two tests differ on one value only, 50 itself, so a wrong choice passes every test that has no mark of exactly 50.

Step through both tests side by side on the same marks. The first preset has 49, 50 and 51, and the last has three marks of exactly 50.

Marks away from 50 get the same answer in both columns. The mark 50 alone passes one test and fails the other, so the final counts differ by exactly the number of 50s. Trying your counter on a mark equal to the boundary is the quickest way to catch this.

## Using the count after the loop

Once the loop exits, the counter or flag holds the final answer. The rest of the program treats it like any other value: printed directly, combined with other output, or tested in a final `if` to choose between two messages. A loop-and-count program usually ends with a line or two that run once, after the loop, never inside it. Printing on every iteration tangles two jobs together.

Indentation is where this pattern most often breaks. The line that increments a counter belongs inside the `if` that names the condition, not at the same indentation as `for`. If `count += 1` lines up with `for`, it runs on every iteration regardless of the condition, and the count comes out too high with no error to point at the mistake.

## A note on speed

The loop does one comparison and at most one addition per item, so the total work grows in direct proportion to how many items there are. The house skeleton keeps that cost real in practice: `sys.stdin.read().split()` reads every token in one call. The loop then pulls values out of that list by position, `pos += 1` at a time, instead of paying a fresh read cost on every line.
