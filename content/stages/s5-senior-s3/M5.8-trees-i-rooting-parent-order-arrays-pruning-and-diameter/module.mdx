Picture a single floor of an office building. A security desk sits at the front and hallways run
out from it. Every room is reachable from the desk by exactly one route: there are no loops that
let you walk back to a room you already passed. That floor plan is a <Term id="tree">tree</Term>,
a connected graph with no cycles. Its $N$ rooms are joined by exactly $N-1$ hallways, and any two
rooms have exactly one path between them.

A general graph, the kind M4.12's adjacency lists were built for, does not promise any of this. It
can hold a cycle, so two rooms might connect by more than one route, and it can hold several
disconnected pieces at once. A tree is what is left once you rule both of those out. That missing
freedom is what makes trees fast to reason about: no route ever needs comparing against another,
because there is only ever one.

## Rooting a tree with an iterative BFS

An unrooted tree has no up or down, only rooms and hallways. Rooting it at one room, the security
desk, turns it into a hierarchy. Every other room gets a parent, the neighbor closer to the desk,
and a depth, its distance from the desk in hallways.

Find both with the same breadth-first search M4.13 used on a grid, now walking an adjacency list
instead. Fill three arrays as the search runs: `parent`, `depth`, and an `order` list recording the
sequence rooms are discovered in. That order is not just bookkeeping. The next section walks it
backward to combine values room by room. It plays the same role a `stack` and `seen` list would in

## Subtree aggregates from the order array

Once a tree is rooted, many questions reduce to combining a value from every room in a
<Term id="subtree">subtree</Term>. How many rooms sit behind a given door? How much equipment do
they hold in total? Walking `order` backward answers both. That walk visits every child before
its parent.

Start every room's size at 1 (itself) and its total at its own item count. Then, for each room in
`order`, from last to first, add its size and total into its parent's. By the time a parent is
reached, every child already folded its numbers in, since children were discovered after their
parent and so sit later in `order`.

## Tree diameter: the longest walk between any two rooms

The <Term id="tree-diameter">diameter</Term> of a tree is the greatest number of hallways between
any two of its rooms, the worst case for someone walking between the farthest pair.

Checking every pair directly means starting a BFS from each of the $N$ rooms and reading off the
largest distance it finds, $O(N)$ work per room, $O(N^2)$ overall. At $N=2 \cdot 10^5$, a bound this
module's problems can reach, that is $4 \cdot 10^{10}$ steps, far past the roughly $10^8$ a second
buys on PyPy. Two BFS passes solve it in $O(N)$ instead. BFS from any room finds the room farthest
from it; BFS from that room finds the room farthest from it in turn, and the distance between them
is the diameter.

Why does the starting room not matter? However far the first search reaches, the room it lands on is
always one end of some diameter. If it were not, the true diameter would have to pass near that room
without touching it. A tree has only one path between any two rooms, so no such detour exists.
Walking from the first search's start toward the true diameter can only get at least as far as
that room did. The second search then only has to look for the other end, and it always finds it,
because that is exactly the room farthest from the first.

## Pruning leaves that do not matter

Some problems mark certain rooms as important and ask for the smallest hallway network still
connecting all of them. Reach it by pruning: repeatedly close any <Term id="leaf">leaf</Term>, a
room whose only hallway leads to one other room, unless that room is marked important. Closing a
room can drop its one neighbor down to a single hallway of its own, so that neighbor becomes a leaf
in turn. Closures move inward one layer at a time until nothing removable is left.

Track each room's <Term id="degree">degree</Term>, its number of live hallways, and start a queue
with every unimportant room of degree 1. Popping a room closes it and lowers its one neighbor's
degree; if that neighbor is now degree 1 and unimportant, it joins the queue. An important room is
never queued, no matter how low its degree falls, so the hallways leading to it always survive.

Scanning the whole tree over and over for closable rooms costs $O(N)$ work per round, and a long
chain can take $O(N)$ rounds, $O(N^2)$ altogether. The queue removes that repeated scanning: every
room is added at most once and popped at most once, so the whole prune runs in $O(N)$.

## Making it fast on PyPy

Every technique here is linear once the adjacency list is built, so the input format decides most of
the running time. Read the whole input at once with `sys.stdin.read().split()`, as every example
above does, rather than calling `input()` per line. Keep `parent`, `depth`, `degree` and the rest as
flat lists indexed by room number, never a dictionary keyed by room; a plain list lookup is one of
the cheapest operations PyPy has. Use a `deque` for every queue, since popping from the front of a
list shifts every remaining entry over. None of these examples recurse, and none should: a tree can
be one long corridor $N$ rooms deep. A recursive walk of it crashes with a `RecursionError` long
before an iterative one would even notice the depth.

Reach for these patterns whenever a problem's input already forms a tree, whether it says so outright
or just guarantees $N-1$ connecting lines with everything reachable from everything else.
