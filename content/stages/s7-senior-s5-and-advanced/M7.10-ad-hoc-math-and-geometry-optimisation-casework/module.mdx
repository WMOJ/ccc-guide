A village has up to 200,000 houses along a straight road, at positions up to 10^9. You dig one well at a whole-number spot, and the cost is the total walking distance from every house to the well. Trying every spot means up to 10^9 spots times 200,000 houses, far past the 10^8 or so operations a solution can afford (M2.9). Nothing standard applies here, no graph and no table to fill. Instead you argue that the best spot must come from a short list, then check only that list. That is casework: name the cases that could win, evaluate each one quickly, and keep the best.

## Why only the houses need checking

Take a spot strictly between two neighboring houses. Move the well one step right. Every house to the left gets one step farther, and every house to the right gets one step closer. The cost changes by the same amount on every step until the well reaches the next house. Between two houses the cost runs along a straight line, and a straight line is lowest at one of its ends. So the best spot is always at a house. That cuts 10^9 cases to N.

## Every position as a case

Sometimes the list of cases is simply every position, and the work is making each one cheap. Take a square grid of numbers, some negative, and find the `k` by `k` window with the largest sum. A grid with N = 1,000 has about 10^6 windows, and adding up `k * k` cells for each costs up to 10^6 times `k * k`. One prefix table, built once as in M4.3, turns each window into four reads. Take the far corner, subtract the strip above and the strip to the left, then add back the corner removed twice.

## A formula instead of a scan

Some problems give you a quantity you can write as a formula. A pen against a long wall has two sides of length `x` and one side of length `w - 2x`, using `w` meters of wire. Its area is `x * (w - 2x)`. If `w` is up to 10^18, you cannot try every `x`, but you do not need to. The area is `-2x^2 + wx`, a parabola that opens downward, so it has one peak, at `x = w / 4`, and falls on both sides.

If `x` had to be whole, the best choice is one of the two whole numbers beside the peak: the floor of `w / 4` and the next one up. If a rule limits `x` to some largest value, a peak beyond that limit means the best `x` is the limit itself. Clamping each candidate into the allowed range handles both cases at once.

## Check the shortcut against brute force

A shortcut like "try the floor of `w / 4`" feels right and is easy to get wrong. The remedy is a brute force that is obviously correct on small inputs, run beside the shortcut on every small value. ## When to reach for casework

Reach for it when a problem asks for the best position or value and the statement has no obvious algorithm. Look for a reason the best case sits at a special place, such as a house, a corner or the peak of a formula. List those places, find a cheap way to evaluate each, and compare against brute force on small inputs.
