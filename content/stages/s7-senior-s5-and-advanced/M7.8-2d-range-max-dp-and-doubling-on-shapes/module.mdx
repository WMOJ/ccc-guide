A weather map is a grid of temperature readings. People keep asking for the hottest reading inside a square patch, such as the 3 by 3 patch whose top-left corner is row 1, column 1. On a 1000 by 1000 map with 100,000 such questions and patches up to 500 cells wide, scanning each patch costs up to 250,000 reads. That is about 2.5 * 10^10 reads in total, far beyond the 10^8 or so simple operations a program can afford (M2.9).

The fix is the doubling idea from M6.12 and the sparse table from M7.3, moved from a line to a grid. The second half of the lesson moves it to triangles, where the same idea needs one new rule.

## A table of blocks, one level per size

Let `table[k][i][j]` be the maximum of the block of `2^k` rows and `2^k` columns whose top-left cell is `(i, j)`. Level 0 is the grid itself. A block of side `2^k` is four blocks of side `2^(k-1)`: the one at `(i, j)` and the three shifted by half the side right, down, or both. A level only has starts where the whole block fits, so level `k` is `n - 2^k + 1` cells on a side.

## Four blocks cover a square

The table holds squares only, so it answers questions about squares: a patch of side `s` whose top-left corner is `(r, c)`. A rectangle that is not a square would need a separate power of two for its height and for its width, which this table does not have.

For a square of side `s`, take `k = s.bit_length() - 1`, the largest `k` with `2^k` at most `s`, as in M7.3. Blocks of side `2^k` fit inside the square. Put one at each corner of the square: the top-left block starts at `(r, c)`, and the others start `s - 2^k` cells to the right, down, or both. The answer is the maximum of those four lookups.

Why four are enough: look at one side first. Two blocks, one starting at the left end and one ending at the right end, cover every column, by the same argument as the one-dimensional sparse table. The same holds for the rows. So any cell has a column that one of the left or right blocks reaches, and a row that one of the top or bottom blocks reaches. The block that combines those two choices contains the cell.

That is harmless for a maximum: reading a value twice does not make it larger. A sum would add those cells twice, which is why M7.3 restricts the two-block trick to maximum, minimum and gcd.

## What the table costs

Each level is a pass over at most `n * n` cells, and there are `n.bit_length()` levels. For a 1000 by 1000 grid that is about 8.3 million entries. A query then costs one `bit_length()` call and four reads, however large the patch is. If every query has the same side, only level `k` is needed, so each level can be dropped once the next is built, which keeps memory near `n * n`.

## Triangles and a rule that changes

Now take a triangle of numbers where row `i` has `i + 1` cells. A triangle of size `m` with its apex at row `i`, column `j` spans `m` rows. Its `r`-th row has `r + 1` cells, starting at column `j`. Suppose you want the maximum of every triangle of one size `K` in a triangle of `N` rows. There are about `(N - K + 1)^2 / 2` of them, and each has about `K^2 / 2` cells. For `N = 3000` and `K = 1500`, scanning them costs about 1.3 * 10^12 reads.

The square trick suggests covering a size-`K` triangle with three triangles at its corners: one at the apex and one at each bottom corner. With squares, blocks of size `2^k` were enough. With triangles, half the size is not.

The middle cell of row 2 belongs to none of the three, so a maximum over the three would miss it. The triangles must overlap. Three triangles of size `m` at the corners of a triangle of size `K` cover it when `K` is at most `2m - 1`, and the bottom triangles then start `K - m` rows down. The exception is `m = 1`, where size 2 is three cells and three single cells cover it with no overlap.

So doubling on triangles builds sizes 1, 2, 3, 5, 9, 17 and so on, each at most `2m - 1` of the one before, and the last step lands exactly on `K` by taking `min(K, 2m - 1)`. For `K = 1500` that is 12 steps. Each step computes a table with one entry per apex, and each entry is one maximum of three lookups in the level below. Across all 12 steps that is about 4.5 * 10^7 entries, instead of 1.3 * 10^12 reads. The figure below builds every level for the 6-row triangle and ends with the maxima of all size-5 triangles.

## When to reach for it

Use this when many queries ask for a maximum, minimum or gcd over one shape, and smaller copies of the shape can cover it with overlap. On a grid of squares that means four blocks of side `2^k`. On a triangle it means three triangles of at least half the size, built by doubling.
