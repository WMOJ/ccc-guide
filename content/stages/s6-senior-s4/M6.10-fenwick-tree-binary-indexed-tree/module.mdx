Back to the rain gauge from M4.3. A station logs the rainfall for each of up to 100,000 days. Two kinds of messages arrive in any order: a correction ("day 3 was 5 mm too low, add 5") and a question ("how much rain fell from day 3 to day 7?"). A <Term id="prefix-sum">prefix sum</Term> array answers the question with one subtraction, but it has no cheap way to take a correction. A <Term id="fenwick-tree">Fenwick tree</Term>, also called a binary indexed tree, takes both in about 17 steps each.

## Why a prefix array fails under corrections

The prefix array stores the total of the first `i` days at `prefix[i]`. A correction to day `p` changes every entry from `prefix[p]` on, because each of them includes that day. With 100,000 days and a correction on day 1, that is 100,000 entries to rewrite. Answering 100,000 mixed messages this way costs on the order of 100,000 times 100,000, ten billion operations. Summing the range directly for every question costs the same.

The tree gets by with few rewrites because no entry stores a full prefix. Each entry stores a short block of days, and a correction only has to reach the blocks that contain that day.

## One block ending at each position

Number the days from 1. In a Fenwick tree, `tree[i]` holds the sum of the block of days that ends at `i` and is as long as the lowest set bit of `i`. The lowest set bit of 6 (binary `110`) is 2, so `tree[6]` covers days 5 and 6. The lowest set bit of 8 (binary `1000`) is 8, so `tree[8]` covers all eight days. Every odd position has lowest set bit 1 and covers just itself.

In Python the lowest set bit of `i` is `i & -i`. For `i = 6`, the negative `-6` is stored as the bits of 6 flipped, plus one, which ends in `...1010`. Its AND with `0110` leaves only the shared lowest bit, `0010`, which is 2.

Each update step moves to a position with a higher lowest set bit, so the walk leaves the array after at most about 17 steps as well.

## Answering a range sum

To total days `l` to `r`, compute `query(r) - query(l - 1)`, the same subtraction as M4.3's `prefix[j + 1] - prefix[i]`. `query(i)` adds `tree[i]`, then moves to `i - (i & -i)`, and stops at 0. Watch it for days 3 to 7: one walk from 7 and one from 2.

<Figure caption="A range sum as two walks down the tree: query(7) adds three blocks, query(2) adds one, and their difference is the answer.">
  <StepThrough frames="visuals/query-walk.frames.json" preset="middle" />
</Figure>

Why do the blocks add up to exactly the first `i` days? The block of `i` is the days just after `i - (i & -i)`, up to `i`. The next block on the walk ends at `i - (i & -i)`, where the previous one began. So the blocks sit end to end with no overlap, from `i` down to 0, and their sum is the prefix total. The "Whole array" preset shows the shortest case, one block, and its `query(0)` is an empty walk.

## Updating one element

A correction to day `p` goes to every block that contains `p`. Starting at `p`, add the change to `tree[i]`, then move to `i + (i & -i)`. Each step goes to the next larger block that contains the previous one, so the walk visits exactly the blocks that contain `p` and stops once `i` passes `n`.

The first two answers, 15 and 13, are the sums of days 1 to 5 and days 3 to 7. The update adds 5 to day 3, which lies inside both ranges, so the same two questions now give 20 and 18. The update takes the change to add, not the new value. To set day 3 to 6 from 1 you call `update(tree, 3, 5)`. Keep days 1-based as well. Position 0 would never move, because `0 & -0` is 0, and the update loop would run forever.

The two loops use only integer additions and comparisons on a flat list, which PyPy runs quickly. With 100,000 messages at roughly 34 loop steps each, that is about 3.4 million iterations. The program reads all its input at once and writes its answers once at the end, instead of calling `input()` and `print()` per message.

## When to reach for it

Reach for a Fenwick tree when values change one at a time and you keep asking for range sums. Counts work too, since a count is a sum of ones. It cuts a prefix down to a range by subtraction. That fits sums, counts and xor, but not a maximum: M4.3 showed that a maximum has no inverse. Range maximum with updates, or updates that touch a whole range at once, belong to the segment tree in M7.1.
