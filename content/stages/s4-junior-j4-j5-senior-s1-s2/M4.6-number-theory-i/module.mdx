A community kitchen has baked 48 muffins and 18 loaves of bread for a fundraiser. Every gift box needs the same number of muffins and the same number of loaves, with nothing left over, and the kitchen wants as many boxes as it can pack. The answer is the largest number dividing both 48 and 18: their <Term id="gcd">greatest common divisor</Term>, or gcd. Every pair shares the divisor 1, so a gcd always exists. The question is finding the largest one without listing every divisor of each.

## Finding the gcd with Euclid's algorithm

Checking every candidate down to 1 works for 48 and 18, but a contest bound might let each number pass a billion. That is one check per candidate, about 10^9 operations, more than a judge allows. Euclid's algorithm needs far fewer steps. Its step count grows with the number of digits, not with the numbers themselves, so a pair near 10^9 finishes in well under a hundred steps.

Divide 48 by 18: quotient 2, remainder 12, since 48 = 18 × 2 + 12. Any number dividing both 48 and 18 also divides 48 − 18 × 2 = 12, and any number dividing both 18 and 12 also divides 48 = 18 × 2 + 12. So (48, 18) and (18, 12) have exactly the same common divisors, and the same gcd. Repeat: (18, 12), then (12, 6), then (6, 0). Once the second number is 0, the first is the answer: gcd(48, 18) = 6.

## Primes and the smallest factor

A <Term id="prime-number">prime number</Term> has exactly two divisors: 1 and itself. Testing by dividing with every candidate from 2 to n − 1 costs about n operations, roughly 10^12 near n = 10^12. That is too slow, but the search can stop at the square root of n without missing anything. Divisors pair up: if d divides n and d is bigger than √n, then n / d is a smaller divisor, below √n. Checking every candidate up to √n catches both halves of every pair. At n = 10^12, √n is only 10^6.

The first candidate that divides n is also its smallest prime factor, since every smaller candidate besides 1 was already ruled out. That search also answers whether n is prime: n is prime exactly when its smallest factor is n itself.

## Finding many primes at once

Some problems ask for every prime up to N, or the smallest factor of many numbers up to N. Testing each alone costs about N times √N operations, near 10^9 at N = 10^6, which is too slow once the sweep repeats.

The <Term id="sieve-of-eratosthenes">sieve of Eratosthenes</Term> finds every prime up to N together, in close to N operations. Starting from 2, a number that is still unmarked has not been ruled out by anything smaller, so it is prime. Mark every multiple of it, starting at its own square and stepping by that prime. A smaller multiple like 2p or 3p has a smaller prime factor and was marked in an earlier pass. What stays unmarked once every candidate up to √N has had its turn is exactly the set of primes.

## Least common multiple

The <Term id="lcm">least common multiple</Term>, or lcm, of two numbers is the smallest positive number both divide evenly, and `gcd.py` above already prints it: `a * b // g`. This works because a × b contains every prime factor of a and of b, counted twice wherever a prime appears in both. Dividing by the gcd removes one copy of the shared part, leaving the smallest number that holds everything both need. For 48 and 18 the gcd is 6, so the lcm is 48 × 18 / 6 = 144, the 144 the trace printed.

## Modular arithmetic and cycles

The mechanics of `%`, including why `a % m` always lands between 0 and m − 1 for a positive m, are covered in M3.8 and do not change here. What number theory adds is a way to answer questions about two independent cycles at once.

Two warning lights blink on their own schedules, one every `a` seconds and the other every `b`, both starting at second 0. A query names a time `t`: when is the first second at or after `t` that both blink together? Their shared period is lcm(a, b), and `t % period` says how far past the last shared blink `t` falls. The answer is `t` itself when that remainder is 0, or `t` plus what is left of the period.

## Keeping it fast on PyPy

Euclid's algorithm and trial division need no speeding up, since both finish in few steps. The sieve touches close to N cells, so two habits matter. It reads input once with `sys.stdin.read().split()`, and it stores the markings in a flat list of booleans, not a dictionary, so each mark is a fast write. One sieve up to N answers a primality question about any number up to N with one lookup, not the √N cost again each time.

A shared factor, a limit on primes, or a cycling count points at gcd, primality testing, the sieve, or modulo.
